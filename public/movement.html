Movement & season

The landscape is not still

Animals move with the seasons, water, and food. A single map of dots hides that. This page adds the dimension a static map can't — carefully, and only where open data honestly allows.

When are these animals recorded?

Every GBIF record carries a month. Counting records by month across the years shows a seasonal rhythm — not a movement track, but a real signal of when animals are observed in this landscape. In the miombo of the Malawi–Zambia border, the year splits into a wet season (roughly November–April) and a dry season (May–October). Crop-raiding conflict is widely reported to peak as crops ripen in the wet-season growing months — the same window when fields hold the most to lose.

Water pulls everything toward it

In the dry season, surface water shrinks to the permanent rivers and the marsh. Animals — and the people fishing, watering livestock, and collecting water — converge on the same shrinking edges. That convergence is where much conflict happens, and it is something open data can show honestly: not as invented animal paths, but as the water features that shape where movement is even possible.

On the main map, rivers and the Vwaza marsh act as a movement affordance layer — the natural corridors water carves through the landscape. Where a crocodile or hippo record sits on a river edge that also draws people, that is a real, data-grounded overlap, not a guess.

What about actual corridors?

The honest answer: this project does not draw them, because it cannot do so truthfully. Real elephant corridors are mapped from GPS collar telemetry — millions of location points — processed with habitat-connectivity models. That data exists for this region (the KAZA elephant tracking work, and corridor studies in Zambian landscapes) but it is held by researchers and parks, not published as open layers.

A named gap, not a fabrication. Drawing invented corridors would look impressive and mislead. Instead, corridors are shown as what open data supports — water and terrain as the affordances that channel movement — with real corridor modelling flagged as the next step that needs collaboration and access to collar data.

Why this matters for food security

The funded project frames this as human-wildlife co-habitation, tied to food security. Season is the link. Fields are most vulnerable in the wet-season growing months; water concentrates animals and people in the dry season. A tool that ignores season flattens a problem that is fundamentally about timing. Showing the seasonal rhythm — from real record-months, alongside where people farm and draw water — is how a data-led view starts to respect that timing rather than erase it.

None of this replaces the incident records, the collar data, or the knowledge of people who live it. It is a different generation's attempt to understand — one that does not think a single map of dots is enough, and that would rather show a real gap than fill it with something decorative.
